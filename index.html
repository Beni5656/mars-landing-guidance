Why this isn't just a physics animation

Bringing a lander from a few kilometers up to a soft, precise touchdown under a bounded, throttleable thrust vector is a real trajectory optimization problem: minimize propellant burned subject to nonlinear dynamics, a maximum tilt angle, throttle limits, and a glide-slope constraint that keeps the approach inside a cone above the pad (visible below as the dashed lines) so the vehicle never grazes terrain.

The trick that makes this solvable onboard, in real time, with a flight computer: lossless convexification — a change of variables that turns the non-convex lower bound on thrust magnitude into a convex second-order-cone program, provably without losing optimality. That result (Acikmese & Ploen, 2007) is the founding method of the Autonomous Controls Lab, led by Behcet Acikmese, which moved to UC Berkeley in 2024 — the same lab whose descendant algorithm, successive convexification (SCvx), now guides real planetary landers and reusable rockets.

Each trajectory here is precomputed by solving the underlying nonlinear program (60-node RK4 direct transcription, IPOPT) for a grid of initial conditions — move the sliders to re-target the solver's output and watch the guidance re-plan the entire burn.
